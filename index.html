Inside the experiment

A body made of tetrahedra

The capybara is a volumetric mesh of — particles and — tetrahedra, cut from a signed-distance capybara: an elliptical section swept along the spine, from a round rump through a deep barrel and a short thick neck to a big boxy head with a blunt muzzle, pressed flat where the belly meets the table and joined smoothly to the haunches, the tucked feet and two small cupped ears. A snapped hex lattice, five tetrahedra per cell, keeps a layer of nodes exactly on the floor. It is solved with XPBD on a fixed 60 Hz step split into 10 substeps; each tetrahedron is pulled toward its best-fit rotated rest shape and keeps its volume. The ears are the firmest, so they flick rather than flop; the clear jelly of the body is the softest.

Two layers

The milky layer is a surface of its own, a hair under the skin, embedded in the same tetrahedra so it bends with the body. Its top is a soft wave along the flank that climbs over the head to make the muzzle. It is lit by light that has crossed the caramel above it, measured from the depth of the key light’s shadow map, so it glows warm where the jelly over it is deep. The — trapped bubbles ride along the same way.

Nose, mouth and cheeks

The nostrils, the line of the mouth, the hollow inside each ear, the rosy cheeks and the faint combed grain of the fur are drawn in the shader from rest-space coordinates, as bumps in the surface normal and tints in the jelly, so they stretch and travel with it. Every few seconds one ear gets a small kick and flicks, the way a capybara’s do.

The yuzu

Each yuzu is a small rigid ball, folded away inside the head until it is dropped. It falls from above the crown, wherever the head has got to, then lands, bounces and rolls; it meets the capybara through the same tetrahedra, carried back to rest space and tested against the shape, and trades momentum with the jelly it hits. The jelly is a little tacky, so a yuzu that lands gently on the flat of the head can stay there.

Light through jelly

WGSL shaders render the floor, the milky layer, the eyes, the bubbles and the yuzu first, then the capybara’s back faces to measure how much jelly each view ray crosses; the front faces refract what lies behind by that thickness and absorb it with Beer–Lambert colour. The thin ears glow when the key light is behind them, and the shadow on the table is tinted by the jelly the light passed through.
